
Henry Hetherington was born in 1792 in Soho, the son of a tailor. He trained as a printer with Luke Hansard, and after a few years in Belgium, he returned to London and worked for Richard Carlile. In the 1820s, he started his own printing and publishing business and joined radical and co-operative groups, influenced by Robert Owen’s ideas. He set up a press in Holborn and published newspapers like Political Economist and Universal Philanthropist.
Hetherington became a leading figure in the “War of the Unstamped”, opposing the government’s newspaper tax that limited working-class access to information. In 1831, he launched the Poor Man’s Guardian, a radical paper advocating universal suffrage and free press. The paper supported working-class causes, including the Tolpuddle martyrs, and circulated widely despite government opposition. Hetherington was imprisoned three times for his work.
His efforts helped lay the groundwork for Chartism. Hetherington remained active in public reform until 1849, when he died of cholera, refusing treatment because of his lifelong teetotal beliefs.
