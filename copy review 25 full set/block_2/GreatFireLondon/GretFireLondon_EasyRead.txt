In 1666, a hot summer and strong wind helped a fire at a bakery near the Monument spread quickly. It burned for four days, destroying 80% of London, including 3,200 houses, 87 churches, and 44 livery halls. The fire reached parts of Fleet Street and the Temple.
Afterwards, there were ideas to rebuild the city with new wide streets, but money was short, so the old street layout stayed. Rebuilding was also expensive because the King ordered new buildings to be made of brick instead of timber, and tenants had to pay rent and rebuild under old leases.
To solve this, the Fire of London Disputes Act created a special Fire Court of 22 judges. They decided who — landlords or tenants — had to rebuild. The court first met in February 1667 at Clifford’s Inn, which had survived the fire.
The City thanked the judges by commissioning large paintings of them for Guildhall. Two of these paintings still survive today.
