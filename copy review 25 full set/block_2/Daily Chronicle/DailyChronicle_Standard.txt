In 1876, a small London newspaper was sold for £30,000. In 1877 it became a national paper called the Daily Chronicle.
The new owner, Edward Lloyd, spent a lot of money to make the paper successful. He built new offices on Fleet Street and bought fast printing machines from America. These machines printed, folded, and cut the paper automatically. This made the newspaper cheaper and quicker to produce.
Lloyd knew people wanted a national newspaper with low-cost advertising. The Chronicle helped workers find jobs and employers find staff. It also printed news from Britain and the rest of the world. At first, there were very few pictures.
The paper had a calm and fair tone. It did not encourage arguments between social groups. It often made fun of politicians but supported social improvement. It became well known for its strong coverage of workers’ issues.
The Daily Chronicle helped educate working people. It mixed news, politics, and culture. It supported Welsh miners during strikes, campaigned for better prisons, and published a letter by Oscar Wilde.
By 1914, 400,000 people bought the paper every day. Sales grew even more during the First World War. Near the end of the war, the editor criticised Prime Minister Lloyd George.
In 1918, Lloyd George bought the Daily Chronicle. The paper was no longer independent and began supporting him politically.
In 1926, Lloyd George sold the paper. The new owners treated it only as a business. The paper soon failed. In 1930, it merged with another newspaper to become the News Chronicle.
