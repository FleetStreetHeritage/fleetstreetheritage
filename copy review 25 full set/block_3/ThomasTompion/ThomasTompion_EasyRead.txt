Thomas Tompion (1639–1713) was born into a family of blacksmiths at a time when clocks were made of iron and were inaccurate. Clockmaking changed in the mid-1600s when the pendulum was introduced, making clocks much more accurate.
Tompion trained as a clockmaker in London and later worked near Fleet Street. He became known as the “Father of English watchmaking” because of his important improvements. In 1695, he took on George Graham as an apprentice, who also became a major innovator.
Early watches were large and bulky, but changing fashion created demand for slimmer pocket watches. Working with scientist Robert Hooke, Tompion helped create the first spring-driven, balance-regulated watch, which greatly improved accuracy.
Tompion’s watches were famous for their quality, skilled workforce, and efficient division of labour. English makers also used the fusee, a device that improved accuracy by controlling the power of the spring, which helped build England’s strong reputation for watchmaking.
Tompion died in 1713. George Graham continued the business, and both men were Fellows of the Royal Society and are buried in Westminster Abbey.
