The Poor Man’s Guardian was started by Henry Hetherington in 1830 during a time of growing radical ideas in England, inspired by the July Revolution in France. It was part of a group of newspapers called the Unstamped, which refused to pay the government’s newspaper stamp tax. These papers faced legal restrictions and trouble distributing their issues, but they pushed hard for lower taxes and more rights for working people.
The paper printed news, court reports, and articles about working-class campaigns and organizations. Hetherington also gave public lectures about the right to vote and unfair taxes. The Poor Man’s Guardian became the most successful of the Unstamped papers, with high circulation and many vendors selling it widely. Its campaigning helped force the government to reduce the Stamp Tax from four pence to one penny in 1836.
Money from the paper supported other working-class causes, including striking workers and families punished for activism. The Poor Man’s Guardian trained many activists and journalists who later joined the Chartist movement. Although it only ran for four years, it had a lasting impact on radical journalism and the fight for workers’ rights in England.
