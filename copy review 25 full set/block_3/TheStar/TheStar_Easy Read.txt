The Star started on 17 January 1888 and sold 126,000 copies on its first day—a world record. It was a four-page evening paper that cost just a halfpenny. Its founder, Irish MP and journalist Thomas Power O’Connor, wanted the paper to speak for ordinary people and stand up to the rich and powerful. Early on, it reported on big stories like the Whitechapel murders and a scandal called the “Times Conspiracy,” which falsely tried to blame Irish leader Charles Stewart Parnell.
Ernest Parke became editor in 1889, and famous writer George Bernard Shaw contributed articles. The paper had popular columns such as Mainly About People and Notes of the Day. By 1895, it was selling more than 150,000 copies a day. Even when it opposed the Boer War and lost readers, the Star kept its independent voice and stood against unfairness and exploitation.
The paper kept growing in the early 1900s. It got bigger, adding more pages, and campaigned on important issues like fighting a high tax on insulin. During the World Wars, the paper had to follow strict censorship and deal with paper shortages, but it remained popular. In the late 1940s, the Star reached its peak, selling over a million copies a day. On the day Princess Elizabeth married Prince Philip in 1947, it sold 1.4 million copies.
The Star came to an end on 17 October 1960 when it was sold and merged with the Evening News, a paper with very different politics. Even so, the Star left a lasting mark with its bold reporting, campaigns for justice, and commitment to ordinary readers.
