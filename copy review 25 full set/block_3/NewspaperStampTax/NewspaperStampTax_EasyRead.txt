The Stamp Tax was a way for the British government to control newspapers. It started in 1712, charging 1 penny per full sheet, ½ penny per half sheet, and 1 shilling per ad. The tax went up and down over time and was finally abolished in 1855.
Newsbooks and pamphlets appeared in England in the 1600s. Printing was tightly controlled by the Star Chamber until 1641. Later laws, like the Licensing of the Press Act 1662, required printers to get approval. These controls ended gradually, with some powers only fully removed in 1765.
The first daily newspaper, the Daily Courant, appeared in 1702. The government saw newspapers as dangerous and introduced the Stamp Tax, which was called a “tax on knowledge.” It started small but increased over the years, especially after 1789 and the Napoleonic wars.
The tax mainly applied to news, not opinion papers, and sometimes publications favorable to the government were exempt. William Cobbett’s Political Register (1817) sold for 2d and reached 40,000 readers.
After the Peterloo Massacre in 1819, the Six Acts made the Stamp Tax stricter, applying it to all newspapers and opinion pieces. Some publications closed, and radical publishers resisted, starting the “War of the Unstamped.”
The struggle ended when ad duty was abolished in 1853 and the Stamp Tax in 1856. A separate tax on paper, started in 1795, ended in 1861.
